# Dostoyevskian Singularity

*From 19th-century existentialism to non-von Neumann chip architectures: how Dostoyevsky’s philosophy of human volition shapes the quest to conquer aging, expand consciousness, and engineer the next generation of brain-computer interfaces.*

***

The 19th-century Russian writer Fyodor Dostoyevsky has had a significant influence on the world, especially on Russian social culture. His works often revolve around suffering and the inner struggles of his characters. In works such as *Crime and Punishment* [[1]](#ref-1) or *Notes from Underground* [[2]](#ref-2), however, the protagonist willingly exposes himself to suffering: Raskolnikov by committing murder, and the Underground Man by continuing to do things he knows he will regret later.

According to Dostoyevsky, people want to experience suffering from time to time and are willing to do the most irrational things to bring about that suffering during the moments when life is going well for them. People do not want to be predictable and want to retain their volition, that is, the ability to make choices for themselves [[2]](#ref-2).

I agree with Dostoyevsky that we often want to feel a momentary sense of self-pity and also receive pity from others. That's what it means to be human. However, it's one thing to experience uncontrollable suffering, and quite another to experience the kind of suffering caused, for example, by listening to emotionally charged music of one's own choosing, such as *On the Nature of Daylight*.

Dostoyevsky was a devout Orthodox Christian and believed in death and suffering partly because of his faith. Contradictorily, living forever is Christianity's whole promise: a resurrected body in a world with no more death. For example, in Revelation 21:4 it is said: "and there shall be no more death," and even further, in Matthew 10:8 Jesus tells his disciples to raise the dead.

On one hand, if someone close to us loses some or all of their senses or ability to move, it's a devastating tragedy which everybody agrees upon. Death, on the other hand, is often considered a natural part of life and sometimes even argued to be as natural as life itself. But that is an equivalence relation: if death is as natural as life itself, then it must be that life is as natural as death itself, right? So wouldn't the ability to choose more life to come support the volition we so desperately need? Consequently, wouldn't our lives be less predictable, since there would be an infinitely greater number of unpredictable future scenarios once death is overcome, and not every life would lead deterministically to death?

In my view, Dostoyevsky's philosophy regarding humanity's innate need for suffering has thus been misinterpreted to this extent. I feel that people value the freedom to choose, but the freedom to choose one's own suffering is limited by accepting death as it is and as one's own opinion, without giving the matter any deeper thought.

Most people would choose death after having lived long enough, say 80 years, but wouldn't choose to die tomorrow. In my opinion, these are two sides of the same coin, just on different scales. How can we know at this stage whether we want to die tomorrow, when we're just one day shy of turning 80? If we could set the exact age at which we'd die right now, what would my 80-year-old self say to the younger version of me who decided on my behalf that I'd die tomorrow? Would we even be able to make this decision when we're young, or would we only then stop to think about it more deeply?

On the other hand, I've also wondered where the line would be drawn once we've conquered aging. If it's possible to live as long as one chooses, should a person then also have the power to decide how to live? The question sounds trivial, but if we factor in the expansion of consciousness, cognition, the senses, or the capacity for empathy, the question becomes more complex.

Let’s take a brief philosophical detour into the concept of consciousness. Consciousness is often difficult to define because subjective experience cannot be measured using objective methods. For example, a spider is capable of weaving the most intricate web structures, but does this outwardly complex behavior make the spider conscious? Can any of us really know whether other people are conscious, or has the physical pattern of others merely settled into a human-like configuration, while consciousness itself has never arisen in the universe except within you? [[3]](#ref-3)

The panpsychistic view of consciousness is that consciousness is a fundamental force inherent in the universe, upon which no other force can exert an influence. If this view is correct, then it would suffice that when a being reaches a certain level of information-processing capacity and appears outwardly to be conscious, it would, as it were, awaken and become activated within this field of consciousness [[3]](#ref-3).

In other words, a rock cannot be conscious because its information-processing capacity is too low, but a mouse, a whale, a hawk, and a human are all conscious beings due to their complex brain structures. In this case, we can therefore assume that when a being appears to be conscious from the outside, it most likely possesses subjective experience in the form we recognize it [[3]](#ref-3).

This state of consciousness is precisely what makes us human and gives life its value. If no one were conscious, it would make no difference whether the world existed at all. Therefore, in order to understand the meaning of life and experience it more deeply, it is essential that humans transcend their biological limitations in their understanding of it.

One scenario for solving this problem could be to connect a person to the cloud via a brain implant, where artificial intelligence would handle the processing of complex data [[3]](#ref-3). The information would be fed back to the brain, where the chip would either project it as sensory data, such as into the field of vision, or directly into the brain, manifesting as a sort of sixth sense.

In this scenario, the computing power would be enormous, and human consciousness could be scaled to its absolute limit. Furthermore, the 1-degree Celsius thermal limit for intracranial computing would remain under control, since most of the computing would take place via a cloud service, not within the implant itself. However, cloud services come with their own data transmission delay, or latency, which would make the experience of this “sixth sense” rather clunky.

Furthermore, the idea raises concerns regarding privacy and free will, because what if the entity managing the cloud network decides to influence the data being transferred through the cloud, or decides to shut it down entirely in order to control the masses? In this doomsday scenario, the emergence of the [Antichrist described by Peter Thiel](https://www.piratewires.com/p/inside-peter-thiels-nashville-antichrist) [[4]](#ref-4) would clearly have come to pass, and in my opinion, creating the conditions that would lead to such a situation would be foolish at this stage if other alternatives exist.

Another option, one that would ensure privacy and security, would be to perform the computations inside the skull. However, this scenario would require a chip architecture that does not operate on the traditional von Neumann architecture, in which the processor and memory are separate, because this architecture generates far too much heat inside the skull, which inevitably leads to gliosis, which in layman's terms means brain scarring.

This dilemma between computing power and privacy shares many characteristics with the ongoing debate over whether an open or closed model is best suited for enterprise use. Arthur Mensch, the CEO and co-founder of Mistral, which develops open models, has argued that businesses don’t want to share their trade secrets and data with entities outside the firm and thus prefer open models. [[5]](#ref-5)

The main difference between open and closed models is whether the provider retains control over the model, and consequently whether computation requires routing data through the cloud, thereby pushing it outside the firm’s infrastructure. Open-weight (and open-source) models provide an option for local computation without the provider ever seeing the data [[5]](#ref-5). I think we will return to this debate in the future once BCI’s become more widespread.

In any case, I feel it is inevitable that humanity will merge with technology, and this transition will require societal changes. I feel that the current way of thinking relies heavily on old, ingrained norms, and we should move beyond them through discussion. I feel that even though people may want to experience suffering and be pitied from time to time, they must also possess the willpower to do so, which is also an important part of Dostoyevsky's theory. Once humanity overcomes this challenge and directs its resources toward conquering aging and all diseases, the next step will be to consider how and to what extent we are prepared to continue living a conscious life.
