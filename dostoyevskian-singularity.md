# Dostoyevskian Singularity

*A brainstorm of ideas from 19th-century existentialism to non-von Neumann chip architectures: how Dostoyevsky’s philosophy of human volition shapes the quest to conquer aging, expand consciousness, and engineer the next generation of brain-computer interfaces.*

***

The 19th-century Russian writer Fyodor Dostoyevsky has had a significant influence on the world, especially on Russian social culture. His works often revolve around suffering and the inner struggles of his characters. In works such as *Crime and Punishment* [[1]](#ref-1) or *Notes from Underground* [[2]](#ref-2), the protagonist willingly exposes himself to suffering: Raskolnikov by committing murder, and the Underground Man by continuing to do detrimental things he knows he will regret later.

According to Dostoyevsky, people want to experience suffering from time to time and are willing to do the most irrational things to bring about that suffering, only to feel alive. His argument rests on the premise, that people do not want to be predictable and are eager to retain their volition, that is, the ability to make choices for themselves [[2]](#ref-2).

I agree with Dostoyevsky that through suffering, we often want to feel a momentary sense of self-pity and also receive pity from others. That’s part of being human. However, it’s one thing to experience uncontrollable suffering, and quite another to experience the kind of suffering caused, for example, by listening to emotionally charged music of one’s own choosing, such as *On the Nature of Daylight*.

On one hand, if someone close to us loses some or all of their senses or ability to move, it’s a devastating tragedy which everybody agrees upon. Death of age, on the other hand, is often considered a natural part of life and sometimes even argued to be as natural as life itself. But that is an equivalence relation: if death is as natural as life itself, then it must also be that life is as natural as death itself, right? So wouldn’t the ability to choose more life to come be as, if not more, natural as choosing to die? Furthermore, wouldn’t the posession of this option support the volition we so desperately need? Consequently, wouldn’t our lives be less predictable, since there would be an infinitely greater number of unpredictable future scenarios once death is overcome, and not every life would lead deterministically to death?

In my view, Dostoyevsky’s philosophy regarding humanity’s innate need for suffering has thus been misinterpreted to this extent. How I see it, is that people value the freedom to choose, but the freedom to choose one’s own suffering is limited by accepting age related suffering and death as it is and as **one’s own opinion**, without giving the matter any deeper thought.

Matter of fact, Dostoyevsky himself, believed in eternal life and rebelled famously against deterministic "2+2=4"-situations [[2]](#ref-2). The clearest biographical data comes from his private journal written the day after his first wife, Maria Dmitrievna, died of tuberculosis, while her body was still lying on the table according to Orthodox custom. Sitting beside her corpse, he wrote: "Masha is lying on the table. Will I see Masha again?" [[6]](#ref-6) In this entry, he explicitly maps out his philosophy of death: human earthly life is a transitional, developmental state, and the "law of the ego" on earth is broken only by the synthesis of an eternal afterlife where consciousness transcends physical laws.

Most people would choose death after having lived long enough, say 80 years, but wouldn’t choose to die tomorrow. In my opinion, these are two sides of the same coin, just on different scales. How can we know at this stage whether we want to die tomorrow, when we’re just one day shy of turning 80? If we could set the exact age at which we’d die right now, what would my 80-year-old self say to the younger version of me who decided on my behalf that I’d die tomorrow? Would we even be able to make this decision when we’re young and would we only then stop to think about it more deeply?

I’ve also wondered where the line would be drawn once we’ve conquered aging. If it’s possible to live as long as one chooses, should a person then also have the power to decide how to live? The question sounds trivial, but if we factor in the expansion of consciousness, cognition, the senses, or the capacity for empathy, the question becomes more complex.

Let’s take a brief philosophical detour into the concept of consciousness. Consciousness is often difficult to define because subjective experience cannot be measured using objective methods. For example, a spider is capable of weaving the most intricate web structures, but does this outwardly complex behavior make the spider conscious? Can any of us really know whether other people are conscious, or has the physical pattern of others merely settled into a human-like configuration, while consciousness itself has never arisen in the universe except within you? [[3]](#ref-3)

The panprotopsychistic view of consciousness is that consciousness is a fundamental force inherent in the universe, upon which no other force can exert an influence. If this view is correct, then it would suffice that when a being reaches a certain level of information-processing capacity and appears outwardly to be conscious, it would, as it were, awaken and become activated within this field of consciousness [[3]](#ref-3).

In other words, a rock cannot be conscious because its information-processing capacity is too low, but a mouse, a whale, a hawk, and a human are all conscious beings due to their complex brain structures. In this case, we can therefore assume that when a being appears to be conscious from the outside, it most likely possesses subjective experience in the form we recognize it [[3]](#ref-3).

An extension to the panprotopsychistic view of consciousness, which would also support the existence of the soul, could be, for example, such that when a human dies, their consciousness deactivates within the field of consciousness and the soul wanders to a new entity, such as a newborn calf. Since the new entity carrying the soul is not made of the same matter as the former, and its information-processing network is wired differently, it cannot activate the memories of the former entity and therefore does not recognize its predecessor, thus severing the continuum of subjective experience between the two different carriers of one conscious soul.

This would, however, imply that the number of conscious souls is finite, and in fact, some precise integer, and that this number is unchangeable. Consequently, another person, or perhaps an animal, may have been a loved one in a previous life. It also implies that we actually do live forever, and that there exists in fact a never-ending cycle of afterlife for the believers after all.

Assuming that only humans can carry consciousness, if there were some exact number of souls in the universe, that would mean that during massacres or some other forms of vast death sprees, there would not be enough newborn babies to carry consciousness compared to those who died. That would mean that the souls would have to hang around for a while, like ghosts, and eventually attach to a new carrier.

Even if animals and plants and whatnot were able to carry consciousness, the fact that the number of souls is some exact number, not some ever-changing amount, breaks the whole idea of panprotopsychism, because then it could be possible that some animal has gained the sufficient information-processing capability, but since there is no free consciousness to attach to it, it would not wake up. Therefore, if the panprotopsychist view is correct, then it must be that new conscious souls form ad infinitum.

The vast state of consciousness is precisely what gives life its value. If no one were conscious, it would make no difference whether the world existed at all. Therefore, it would be highly beneficial, I think, that humans transcend their biological limitations to expand consciousness.

Furthermore, human brains can process vast amounts of information, but their output, such as speech production and writing, is slow. If artificial intelligence advances at an exponential rate, as Ray Kurzweil argues in _The Singularity Is Near_ [[7]](#ref-7), human communication with AI will eventually become too slow, inevitably leaving us out of the decision-making loop. Just as chimpanzees can communicate effectively among themselves from their own perspective, communication between humans and chimpanzees feels profoundly inefficient to us when compared to communicating with another human. And as Elon Musk argued in an interview with _The Economist_, we would not want chimpanzees to lead us either [[8]](#ref-8).

Humans have a strong tendency to project their own psychology onto other agents (anthropomorphism). It is often intuitively assumed that as an agent’s intelligence grows, it inevitably becomes wiser, more empathetic, more ethical, or more noble. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, the philosopher behind the French Revolution, held that once a person is sufficiently educated, their innate goodness can be harnessed [[9]](#ref-9). This implies either that humans are naturally benevolent and corrupted by circumstance, or that purely increasing intelligence also increases benevolence. In either case, the outcome is the same: more knowledge and capacity for abstraction equals more benevolence.

According to Nick Bostrom’s orthogonality thesis, intelligence and motivation do not go hand in hand; in fact, he argues that they are orthogonal to each other [[10]](#ref-10). Bostrom famously presented the idea of how removing humans from the decision-making loop could result, for instance, in a scenario where an AI specializes in producing paperclips and does everything in its power to stay dedicated to that single task [[11]](#ref-11). In this scenario, the entire biosphere would be mere raw material to the AI, and all the matter in the world would ultimately be converted into paperclips. In such a case, the AI’s intelligence would be vastly superior to human intellect, but its benevolence, from the perspective of humanity, would run on completely different tracks.

Therefore, in order to remain in the decision-making loop, it is crucial that human cognition and communication capabilities keep pace, at least to some degree, with AI, as we cannot take for granted that an artificial intelligence will necessarily have our best interests at heart.

One tool for expanding consciousness could be to connect a person to the cloud via a brain implant, where artificial intelligence would handle the processing of complex data. The information would be fed back to the brain, where the chip would either project it as sensory data, such as into the field of vision, or directly into the brain, manifesting as a sort of sixth sense.

In this scenario, the computing power would be enormous, and human consciousness could be scaled to its absolute limit. Furthermore, the 1-degree Celsius thermal limit for intracranial computing would remain under control, since most of the computing would take place via a cloud service, not within the implant itself. However, cloud services come with their own data transmission delay, or latency, which would make the experience of this “sixth sense” rather clunky if a lot of computation power is required.

Furthermore, the idea raises concerns regarding privacy and free will, because what if the entity managing the cloud network decides to influence the data being transferred through the cloud, or decides to shut it down entirely in order to control the masses? In this doomsday scenario, the emergence of the [Antichrist described by Peter Thiel](https://www.piratewires.com/p/inside-peter-thiels-nashville-antichrist) [[4]](#ref-4) would clearly have come to pass, and in my opinion, creating the conditions that would lead to such a situation would be foolish at this stage if other alternatives exist.

Another option, one that would ensure privacy and security, would be to perform the computations inside the skull. However, this scenario would require a chip architecture that does not operate on the traditional von Neumann architecture, in which the processor and memory are separate, because this architecture generates far too much heat inside the skull, which inevitably leads to gliosis, which in layman’s terms means brain scarring.

This dilemma between computing power and privacy shares many characteristics with the ongoing debate over whether an open or closed model is best suited for enterprise use. Arthur Mensch, the CEO and co-founder of Mistral, which develops open models, has argued that businesses don’t want to share their trade secrets and data with entities outside the firm and thus prefer open models. [[5]](#ref-5)

The main difference between open and closed models is whether the provider retains control over the model, and consequently whether computation requires routing data through the cloud, thereby pushing it outside the firm’s infrastructure. Open-weight (and open-source) models provide an option for local computation without the provider ever seeing the data [[5]](#ref-5). I think we will return to this debate in the future once BCI’s become more widespread.

In any case, I feel it is inevitable that humanity will merge with technology, and this transition will require societal changes. I feel that the current way of thinking relies heavily on old, ingrained norms, and we should move beyond them through discussion. I feel that even though people may want to experience suffering and be pitied from time to time, they must also possess the willpower to do so, which is also an important part of Dostoyevsky's theory. Once humanity overcomes this challenge and directs its resources toward conquering aging and all diseases, the next step will be to consider how and to what extent we are prepared to continue living a conscious life.
