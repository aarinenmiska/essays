# Dostoyevskian Singularity

*A brainstorm of ideas from 19th-century existentialism to non-von Neumann chip architectures: how Dostoyevsky’s philosophy of human volition shapes the quest to conquer aging, expand consciousness, and engineer the next generation of brain-computer interfaces.*

***

## Part I: Introduction

The 19th-century Russian writer Fyodor Dostoyevski has had a significant influence on the world, especially on Russian social culture. His works often revolve around suffering and the inner struggles of his characters. In works such as _Crime and Punishment_ [[1]](#ref-1) or _Notes from Underground_ [[2]](#ref-2), the protagonist willingly exposes himself to suffering: Raskolnikov by committing "morally rightful" murder, and the Underground Man by continuing to do irrational things he knows he will regret later.

According to Dostoyevski, people want to experience suffering from time to time and are willing to do the most irrational things to bring about that suffering. His argument rests on the premise that people do not want to be predictable and are eager to retain their volition, that is, the ability to make choices for themselves [[2]](#ref-2). Dostoyevski viewed the Crystal Palace, built in 1851 to London, as the archetype of a deterministic, fully optimized society [[2]](#ref-2).

Although curing aging and optimizing the world for a technological singularity are the ultimate Crystal Palace to Dostoyevski, even he felt the primal human refusal to accept death. The clearest biographical data comes from his private journal written the day after his first wife, Maria Dmitrievna, died of tuberculosis, while her body was still lying on the table according to Orthodox custom. Sitting beside her corpse, he wrote: "Masha is lying on the table. Will I see Masha again?" [[3]](#ref-3) In this entry, he refuses to accept physical death as the final word on consciousness and hints at a belief in an afterlife where consciousness transcends physical limits.

My thesis is that for humanity to keep the freedom to choose our own struggles, humanity must break out of two deterministic battlefronts: biological determinism and technological determinism.

## Part II: Breaking Biological Determinism

I agree with Dostoyevski that it is part of human nature to want some friction in life and suffer because of it. It is also part of human nature to feel a momentary sense of self-pity for one's own suffering, be it imagined or real, and maybe even receive some pity from others. However, it’s one thing to experience uncontrollable suffering, and quite another to experience the kind of suffering caused, for example, by listening to emotionally charged music of one’s own choosing, such as _On the Nature of Daylight_.

On one hand, if a loved one loses some or all of their senses or ability to move, it’s a devastating tragedy which everybody agrees upon universally. Death from old age, on the other hand, is often considered a natural part of life and sometimes even argued to be as natural as life itself. But blindness and paralysis are completely natural as well, yet nobody argues we should accept them passively. Aging is simply the slow, systemic loss of those same capabilities. Therefore naturalness is irrelevant to whether something is worth fighting against.

Death is also argued to give meaning to life, because it creates a sense of urgency. What I argue is that death in itself doesn't provide meaning but volition does. Giving people the option to extend life does not ban anyone from choosing an end, it simply removes a forced deterministic expiration date and vastly multiplies the unpredictable futures to carry out volition-induced actions.

So in my view, the problem is that people value the freedom to choose, but the freedom to choose one’s own suffering is limited by accepting age-related suffering and death as it is and as **one’s own opinion**, without giving the matter any deeper thought. In fact, most people would choose death after having lived long enough, say 80 years, but wouldn’t choose to die tomorrow. In my opinion, these are two sides of the same coin, just on different scales. How can one know at this stage whether they want to die tomorrow, when they’re just one day shy of turning 80? If one could set the exact age at which they’d die, what would their 80-year-old self say to the younger version of them who decided on their behalf that they’d die tomorrow? Realistically, would anyone even be able to make this decision when they’re young and would they only then stop to think about it more deeply?

## Part III: Consciousness

I’ve also wondered where the line would be drawn once we’ve conquered aging. If it’s possible to live as long as one chooses, should a person then also have the power to decide how to live? The question sounds trivial at first, but if we factor in the expansion of consciousness, cognition, the senses, or the capacity for empathy, the question becomes more complex.

Consciousness is often difficult to define because subjective experience cannot be measured using objective methods. For example, a spider is capable of weaving the most intricate web structures, but does this outwardly complex behavior make the spider conscious? Matter of fact, can any of us really know whether other people are conscious, or has the physical pattern of others merely settled into a human-like configuration, while consciousness itself has never arisen in the universe except within you? [[4]](#ref-4)

The panprotopsychistic view of consciousness is that consciousness is a fundamental force inherent in the universe, upon which no other force can exert an influence. If this view is correct, then it would suffice that when a being reaches a certain level of information-processing capacity and appears outwardly to be conscious, it would, as it were, awaken and become activated within this field of consciousness [[4]](#ref-4). In other words, a rock cannot be conscious because its information-processing capacity is too low, but a mouse, a whale, a hawk, and a human are all conscious beings due to their complex brain structures. In this case, we can therefore assume that when a being appears to be conscious from the outside, it most likely possesses subjective experience in the form we recognize it [[4]](#ref-4). If consciousness scales with information-processing complexity, then biological decay reduces it to zero while cognitive augmentation scales it upward.

If no one were conscious, it would make no difference whether the world existed at all. Therefore, the value representing the existence of the world is tied directly to the level of consciousness it encompasses. Whether consciousness emerges from reaching a certain information-processing capacity or something deeper, we have zero verifiable guarantee of subjective continuity after biological deactivation. Therefore biological decay and death permanently destroy the fundamental value of the world and more closely, life.

Such a silent universe with no afterlife is the exact premise behind nihilism described by Dostoyevski: if there is no afterlife and the universe is indifferent to our actions, everything is permitted [[5]](#ref-5). In *Notes from Underground*, Dostoyevski argues that humans want to stick out their tongues at the Crystal Palace to prove their freedom against an optimized and deterministic order [[2]](#ref-2). In the context of mortality, treating death as an act of defiance or spite, as if you are rattling some cosmic authority or god, fails on its own terms. Rebellion requires an authority to defy, and a conscious rebel left standing to defy it. In a silent, godless universe, there is no god to rattle, and deactivation erases the rebel too. To borrow Raskolnikov's accusation from *Crime and Punishment*: "you have mortified and betrayed yourself for nothing." [[1]](#ref-1) The only way to rebel against such an indifferent universe is to stay conscious, expand one's mind, and find meaning where the universe offers none.

## Part IV: Breaking Technological Determinism

Expanding consciousness could start as a philosophical rebellion against a silent universe but it quickly becomes a practical requirement for survival once AI enters the picture. Human brains can process vast amounts of information, but their output, such as speech production and writing, is slow. If artificial intelligence advances at an exponential rate, as Ray Kurzweil argues in _The Singularity Is Near_ [[6]](#ref-6), human communication with AI will eventually become too slow, inevitably leaving us out of the decision-making loop. Just as chimpanzees can communicate effectively among themselves from their own perspective, communication between humans and chimpanzees feels profoundly inefficient to us when compared to communicating with another human. And as Elon Musk argued in an interview with _The Economist_, we would not want chimpanzees to lead us either [[7]](#ref-7).

Humans have a strong tendency to project their own psychology onto other agents (anthropomorphism). It is often intuitively assumed that as an agent's intelligence grows, it inevitably becomes wiser, more empathetic, more ethical, or more noble. According to Nick Bostrom's orthogonality thesis, intelligence and motivation do not go hand in hand. In fact, he argues that they are orthogonal to each other [[8]](#ref-8). Bostrom famously presented the idea of how removing humans from the decision-making loop could result, for instance, in a scenario where an AI specializes in producing paperclips and does everything in its power to stay dedicated to that single task [[9]](#ref-9). In this scenario, the entire biosphere would be mere raw material to the AI, and all the matter in the world would ultimately be converted into paperclips. In such a case, the AI's intelligence would be vastly superior to human intellect, but its benevolence, from the perspective of humanity, would run on completely different tracks.

Therefore, in order to remain in the decision-making loop, it is crucial that human cognition and communication capabilities keep pace at least to some degree with AI, as we cannot take for granted that an artificial intelligence will necessarily have our best interests at heart.

Bostrom's orthogonality thesis also hints that high intelligence does not automatically require independent agency or self-directed goals [[8]](#ref-8). Although agentic AI integrated into human cognition could hold an independent utility function and therefore pose a risk for humanity to lose control, a non-agentic inferential AI could do something different. By expanding human bandwidth and adding AI as a sort of "sixth sense" without importing an independent agent's goals, the integration with AI would be much more like adding a new sensory cortex. I mean, imagine how many unconscious computations happen every millisecond because of the sensory data one's retina, visual cortex and the cerebellum handle. Yet nobody argues that having eyes violates their free will. Vision only expands one's option pool, leaving the final decision to the human. In the context of brain-computer interfaces, adding an AI sixth sense would be equivalent to scaling up the sensory and computational substrate that human volition operates on.

## Part V: The BCI Trilemma

One proposed solution to expanding consciousness is to connect a person to the cloud via brain implant, and artificial intelligence would handle the processing of complex data inside a data center. The information would be fed back to the brain, where the chip would either project it as sensory data, such as into the field of vision, or directly into the brain, manifesting as a sixth sense. In this scenario, the computing power would be enormous, and human consciousness could be scaled to its absolute limit. Furthermore, the 1-degree Celsius thermal limit for intracranial computing would remain under control, since most of the computing would take place via a cloud service, not within the implant itself. However, cloud services come with their own data transmission delay, or latency, which would make the experience of the sixth sense rather clunky.

Furthermore, the idea raises concerns regarding privacy and free will, because even a purely perceptual sixth sense would become a Crystal Palace control mechanism if an external actor controls the feed. If a corporation or state can subtly bias what the sixth sense highlights or hides, they could steer one's choices without ever touching anyone's free will directly. In this doomsday scenario, the emergence of the Antichrist described by Peter Thiel [[10]](#ref-10) would clearly have come to pass, and in my opinion, creating the conditions that would lead to such a situation would be foolish at this stage if other alternatives exist.

A Second option would be to do computations on an external local device, which then sends the data back to the brain implant. Such a solution has a severe compute ceiling compared to data centers, but at least privacy is protected while intracranial heat is still under control.

A third option would be to perform the computations inside the skull. However, intracranial computation would require a chip architecture that does not operate on a traditional von Neumann architecture, in which the processor and memory are separate, because such chips generate far too much heat inside the skull, which inevitably leads to gliosis if any meaningful computations are desired. The latency would be lowest of the three and the user would have full sovereignty, thus protecting the user's privacy the most out of the three. However, the computational ceiling is even lower than for an external local device.

Closing the gap between efficient local hardware and centralized cloud compute is the exact engineering bottleneck ahead, just like in enterprise AI today. In fact, this trilemma shares many characteristics with the ongoing debate over whether an open or closed model is best suited for enterprise use. Arthur Mensch, the CEO and co-founder of Mistral, which develops open models, has argued that businesses don’t want to share their trade secrets and data with entities outside the firm and thus prefer open models [[11]](#ref-11). The main difference between open and closed models is whether the provider retains control over the model, and consequently whether computation requires routing data through the cloud, thereby pushing it outside the firm’s infrastructure. Open-weight (and open-source) models provide an option for local computation without the provider ever seeing the data [[11]](#ref-11). I think we will return to a similar debate in the future once BCIs become more widespread.

## Part VI: Volition Beyond Biology

Clinging to old, ingrained norms while waiting for the future to unfold is already a choice. Accepting aging as an untouchable fact of nature surrenders our volition to biological determinism, while ignoring how artificial intelligence and brain-computer interfaces are built surrenders it to external algorithms or centralized cloud providers. Both paths lead straight to the deterministic predictability that Dostoyevski rebelled against. Given current momentum, these technologies are likely coming, but the form they take is still up to us.

Because artificial intelligence is advancing rapidly right now, conquering aging and securing cognitive sovereignty cannot be treated as separate steps decades apart. They are two halves of the same project. Without overcoming biological aging, any expansion of consciousness remains temporary and ends in involuntary deactivation. Without expanding our bandwidth and building a perceptual sixth sense on local, open hardware, living longer would simply turn us into long-lived chimpanzees locked inside someone else's Crystal Palace.

Dostoyevski was right that humans need the freedom to choose their own struggles, and even the freedom to act irrationally. For that volition to hold any weight, we actually have to possess the willpower to claim it, rather than letting biological decay or a centralized network dictate our trajectory. Overcoming aging while keeping our cognitive interfaces local and sovereign is what keeps genuine human agency intact.

Once humanity stops treating biological death as mandatory and secures the sovereignty of its own minds, the real challenge begins. At that point, we are finally free from the ceilings imposed by nature and external control. The question left standing is how far we are actually prepared to expand our consciousness, and what kind of meaning we choose to create in an indifferent universe once nothing forces our hand.
